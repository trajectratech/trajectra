# Business growth strategy

## Start here: the site cannot currently convert

Before any acquisition tactic, understand the constraint. The site has no case
studies, no named team, no proof of process, and no tracked conversion events. Sending
traffic to it wastes the traffic. **Fix conversion before buying attention** —
every recommendation below assumes the trust assets in the brand and UI/UX
reports land first.

Also note: nothing here is measurable today. There are no GA4 conversion events
on the booking links or the contact form, so the current conversion rate is
literally unknown. That is the first thing to fix, in an afternoon.

## Attracting international clients

Ranked by realistic return for a small firm with no reputation yet.

### 1. Outbound to a narrow, specific segment (fastest)

Organic SEO on a young domain takes 9–18 months. Outbound works in weeks — but
only if it is narrow.

Pick **one** segment and go deep. For example: *seed-stage European fintechs
that raised in the last 6 months and have fewer than 5 engineers.* They have
money, urgency, and a hiring problem, and they are enumerable from Crunchbase
and EU-Startups.

The message that works is not "we do custom software." It is a specific,
unsolicited observation about *their* product — a bug, a performance problem, a
missing flow — with a note that you fixed something similar. Ten of those a week
beats a thousand templated emails, and it will not get the domain flagged.

### 2. Marketplaces (fastest revenue, worst margin)

Toptal, Gun.io, Upwork Enterprise, Clutch, and the European equivalents. Margins
are poor and the platform owns the relationship — but the first ten reviews are
worth more than the margin sacrificed. **Treat this as a reputation purchase
with a 12-month exit.**

### 3. Partner channel (best long-term margin)

Design studios, fractional-CTO consultancies, and no-code agencies constantly
hit work they cannot deliver. A standing 10–15% referral arrangement with five
such firms produces warm, pre-qualified leads at near-zero acquisition cost.
This is the most underrated channel for an agency of this size.

### 4. Content and tools (slowest, compounds)

The `/tools/` directory plus the five decision-intent posts in the SEO audit.
Nothing here pays for 6–9 months, then it pays indefinitely.

### 5. Communities

Not spam. Genuine, sustained participation in the places where the target
segment already talks — indie hackers, relevant subreddits, specific Slack and
Discord communities. Slow, and only works if the participation is real.

**Do not** buy Google Ads for "software development company" yet. Those clicks
run $15–40 in the US/EU and land on a page with no proof. It is a way to spend
money on nothing.

## Pricing

The current model is invisible, which almost certainly means it is
hourly-and-negotiated. That is the worst structure available: it caps income at
hours, makes efficiency a punishment, and invites haggling.

**Move to three tiers:**

| Model | Structure | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed-scope projects** | Priced by outcome, not hours. Agreed in full before work starts. | New clients, first engagement |
| **Retainer / embedded team** | Per engineer per month, 3-month minimum | The revenue you actually want |
| **Productised diagnostics** | 5-day Audit · 2-week Modernisation Assessment | Top of funnel |

**Three specific changes:**

1. **Publish the model and the durations, even though the figures stay
   private.** You have chosen not to publish prices — see "Qualifying without a
   price page" below for how to recover most of the benefit anyway. The change
   that matters regardless is moving off hourly billing onto fixed-scope and
   retainer structures.
2. **Price against the buyer's alternative, not your cost.** A London startup
   compares Trajectra to a $9k/month London contractor, not to Lagos salaries.
   Pricing off local cost leaves most of the value on the table and — this is
   the counterintuitive part — *lowers* perceived quality. A price far below the
   market reads as a warning, not a bargain.
3. **Retainers are the goal.** Project revenue is a treadmill; retainer revenue
   is a business with a valuation. Every project should have a defined path into
   a maintenance or embedded-team retainer.

## Service packaging

Three tiers, in escalating commitment:

- **Diagnose** — Audit / Assessment. Days. Low commitment, high conversion into
  the next tier.
- **Build** — MVP Sprint or a defined project. Weeks. Fixed scope, fixed price.
- **Operate** — embedded team or maintenance retainer. Monthly. Ongoing.

Design the funnel so each tier naturally sells the next. The audit report should
end with a costed build proposal; the build handover should end with a
maintenance quote.

## Qualifying without a price page

Not publishing prices is a defensible call, but it moves the entire qualifying
burden onto your calendar. Without a compensating mechanism, the predictable
outcome is a diary full of calls with people who were never going to buy.

The mechanism, in order of effort:

1. **A required budget-range question on the booking form.** Four bands, plus
   "not sure yet". Ten minutes of work, and it is the single highest-return item
   in this document relative to effort. It also gives you data: the distribution
   of answers tells you what the market thinks you are worth.
2. **Publish the engagement minimums.** "Projects from 8 weeks. Retainers with a
   three-month minimum." Self-selects out the smallest enquiries.
3. **Publish durations for the productised offers.** 5-day Audit, 10-week MVP
   Sprint. Concrete without being a number.
4. **State the commercial model plainly** — fixed price agreed before work
   starts, change orders for scope changes, IP transferred on payment.

Track the effect: **lead→call rate should fall and call→proposal rate should
rise.** If call→proposal stays flat after adding the budget question, the
problem is lead source, not qualification.

## Lead generation

- **Track conversions first.** GA4 events on every booking link and the form.
  Without this you cannot tell which channel works, and every decision below is
  guesswork.
- **Make the booking link the primary CTA** everywhere, on a Trajectra-owned
  domain (`trajectra.com/book`).
- **Add a lead magnet with real substance** — a "Technical Due Diligence
  Checklist" or "What an MVP Actually Costs" PDF. Email capture with something
  genuinely worth the address.
- **Add live chat only if someone will actually answer it.** An unanswered chat
  widget is worse than none.
- **Exit-intent is not worth the annoyance** at this traffic level.

## Conversion improvements, in order of impact

1. Case studies with numbers.
2. Named team with photos.
3. Published engagement models and contract terms (not figures).
4. A written process — "what happens in the first two weeks."
5. An FAQ answering timezone, contracts, IP, and payment.
6. One CTA, repeated, everywhere.
7. Social proof near every CTA, not in a separate section.

## Authority building

- **Write from the team, not from marketing.** One deep technical post a month
  beats four shallow ones.
- **Speak.** Conference talks and podcast appearances convert far above their
  audience size because they transfer trust directly.
- **Open source.** A GitHub organisation with real work is the most efficient
  credibility signal an engineering firm has.
- **Publish the failures too.** "What we got wrong building X" is read more, and
  trusted more, than another success story. Almost no agency will do this, which
  is exactly why it works.

## Long-term

**Year 1 — Credibility.** Ten delivered projects, five public case studies, a
verified Clutch profile with real reviews, a functioning tools directory.
Revenue: services. Goal: become verifiable.

**Year 2 — Leverage.** Retainer revenue exceeds project revenue. Partner channel
producing consistent referrals. Ranking for long-tail commercial terms. One
product in market with paying customers.

**Year 3 — Compounding.** Product MRR covers fixed costs, which means the
services business can start refusing bad work — the point at which margins and
quality both improve.

## The metrics that matter

Track: qualified leads per month · lead→call rate · call→proposal rate ·
proposal→close rate · average contract value · **revenue per employee** ·
retainer share of revenue · client concentration.

Ignore: page views, social followers, keyword rankings in isolation, Lighthouse
scores. Those are inputs. The number that decides whether this is a good
business is **revenue per employee**, and the number that decides whether it is
a *durable* one is **retainer share of revenue**.
